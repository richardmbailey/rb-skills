# Editorial patterns

Use these patterns to diagnose prose in context. Their presence is not proof that text was generated by AI, and no single form should be removed mechanically.

## Meaning and evidence

### Inflated significance

The draft treats an ordinary fact as a pivotal moment, enduring testament, broad shift, or symbol without supporting that interpretation. State the concrete fact and its demonstrated consequence.

### Vague authority

The draft relies on unnamed experts, observers, reports, or critics. Preserve the limited attribution if it matters, remove it if it contributes nothing, or flag the need for a source. Never invent a source.

### Unsupported specificity

The prose would benefit from a date, example, quotation, or measured result, but the source does not contain one. Mark the gap rather than supplying a plausible detail.

### Promotional framing

Praise, superlatives, and claims of novelty substitute for evidence. Replace promotional phrasing with observable characteristics. Preserve supported attributed content without carrying over its marketing language; if it appears in a direct quotation, follow the quotation rule below.

### Empty analysis

Trailing participial phrases or abstract commentary claim that a fact highlights, reflects, showcases, fosters, or underscores something without explaining a supported relationship. State the relationship directly or remove the empty inference.

### Lost qualification

Compression or stronger wording erases uncertainty, scope, exceptions, or attribution. Restore the original epistemic status even if the sentence becomes less emphatic.

## Structure and movement

### Canned opening or conclusion

The text begins with generic throat-clearing or ends with an upbeat summary that adds no information. Begin with the subject and end on the final substantive point.

### Slogan-like or marketing-style framing

The draft packages information as a memorable slogan, tagline, punch line, aphorism, or marketing claim. It may appear as a short sentence before the explanation or as rhetoric embedded within an otherwise substantive sentence. Common forms include "X is not Y", "This is about X, not Y", "more than X", "not just X", "where X meets Y", and polished declarations designed to sound decisive rather than explain the subject.

Remove the slogan-like wording completely. Do not preserve it by joining it to the explanatory sentence or by replacing it with a new slogan. Identify the supported meaning underneath the rhetoric and state that meaning directly through the concrete mechanism, claim, evidence, or limitation.

Do not target short sentences merely because they are short. Keep a short sentence when it communicates a substantive result, changes the direction of the argument, or provides a necessary transition. Remove it when its function is rhetorical packaging. The final wording should be plain, informative, and free of slogans or marketing language.

A necessary factual contrast is not a slogan merely because it uses words such as "but" or "not". Keep the contrast when removing it would change the meaning, but explain the concrete difference rather than presenting it as a memorable formula.

If slogan-like wording appears inside a direct quotation, do not edit the quoted words. Omit the quotation only when its supported meaning and attribution can be preserved elsewhere. If the quotation is material or explicitly protected, ask whether it may be omitted or whether the no-slogan requirement should change. Do not produce a revision with an unresolved conflict.

For example, replace "That distinction is about lineage, not truth" with the actual limitation: a hash can show that a passage matches the recorded source, but it cannot show whether the source is current or relevant. The replacement explains the distinction without carrying the slogan into a longer sentence.

### Formulaic balance

Repeated three-part lists, negative parallelism, or symmetrical contrasts make the argument sound preassembled. Keep genuine conceptual groupings; vary or simplify decorative ones.

### Outline residue

Sections such as challenges, future prospects, key takeaways, or conclusion exist because a template expects them rather than because the material needs them. Merge, rename, or remove them when the genre permits.

### Repetitive rhythm

Sentences repeatedly use the same length, clause order, transition, or rhetorical turn. Restructure around the logic of the content rather than adding arbitrary variation.

### Synonym cycling

The draft renames the same subject repeatedly to avoid lexical repetition. Use the clearest stable term when repetition improves coherence.

## Language and presentation

### Abstract or inflated diction

Words about landscapes, journeys, tapestries, transformation, importance, or innovation obscure the actual claim. Prefer the precise domain term or ordinary verb. Do not ban a word that is accurate in context.

### Copula avoidance

Phrases such as "serves as", "stands as", "represents", "features", or "boasts" replace a clearer "is" or "has". Simplify when the elaborate construction adds no meaning.

### Filler and layered hedging

Phrases such as "in order to", "it is important to note", or several stacked modal words delay the point. Remove redundancy while preserving the strongest necessary qualification.

### Chatbot residue

The deliverable includes greetings, praise, offers to continue, knowledge-cutoff disclaimers, or commentary about fulfilling the prompt. Remove material that is not part of the intended document.

### Formatting habits

Em dashes, bold lead-ins, title case, emojis, vertical lists, or frequent headings may become mannered through repetition. Follow the medium and house style; do not treat typography as authorship evidence.

## Voice check

After revising, ask:

- Does the prose fit the audience and medium?
- Can every fact, opinion, feeling, and personal statement be traced to supplied material?
- Does the level of formality match the available voice evidence?
- Have natural contractions, humour, fragments, or asides been retained when they belong to the author?
- Has the revision become generically punchy, confessional, or conversational without permission?
- Are headings, list labels, captions, callouts, and closing lines also free of slogans and marketing language?
- Would any changed punctuation or formatting improve this document even if AI authorship were irrelevant?
